Proxies in Hiring Algorithms

Stripped-down resumes can't stop algorithms from finding clues that replicate old prejudices.

Proxies

Even when algorithms don't know demographic information about their applicants, the bias in hiring practices still shines through because of the use of proxies for characteristics. Researchers attempted to remove the bias from the computer program by limiting the identifying information that the computer would receive. Instead of feeding the computer an applicant’s entire resume, the computer was fed a resume without identifying characteristics like race, gender, name, etc. However, the computer algorithm continued to be biased by making connections between information like ‘men’s lacrosse’ and ‘white man.’

“If the system notices that recruiters happen to interact more frequently with white men, it may well find proxies for those characteristics (like being named Jared or playing high school lacrosse) and replicate that pattern.”

—Miranda Bogen, Harvard Business Review (2019)

This type of connection is called a proxy, when one piece of information is replaced by another, but at the base level it represents the same thing. Even though the algorithm can’t discriminate directly based on race and gender, it can notice patterns and reproduce them. The algorithm could no longer tell that white men were hired more, but it could see that resumes with the key words ‘men’s lacrosse’ were hired more than those without them. Even though the algorithm wasn’t fed specific demographic information, the computer still noticed that there was bias toward majority groups. Not only do hiring algorithms reward majority groups, they also openly punish people from minority groups. Jeffrey Dastin found that an algorithm researched by Amazon “penalized resumes that included the word ‘women’s,’ as in ‘women’s chess club captain,’ and it downgraded graduates of two all-women’s colleges.” The algorithm noticed that a group of people were hired less, and even though it didn’t know that group was women, it did notice ‘women’ as a keyword and disadvantaged them significantly in an attempt to replicate the data that it was given. Algorithms like these contribute substantially to the biases in hiring practices, and until we have the technology to make them unbiased, there needs to be laws in place to protect minority groups from being discriminated against.
